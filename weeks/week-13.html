Week 13: The future of IT: AI, quantum, and zero trust

AI is changing both attacks and defenses, zero trust is replacing the old castle-and-moat model, and quantum computing is forcing a change in encryption. What does that mean for your job in 2030?

Explore

AI on both sides

Defenders use AI to summarize alerts, spot unusual behavior, write detection rules, and speed up investigations. Attackers use it to write convincing phishing in any language, clone voices, and automate reconnaissance. Organizations also need people who can secure AI systems themselves. The skills that rise in value: judging whether AI output is correct, understanding the systems underneath, and communicating clearly.

Zero trust

The old model trusted anything inside the network, like a castle with a moat. Zero trust assumes no user or device is trusted by default, inside or outside. Every request is checked: who is asking, from what device, for what, and whether that makes sense right now. CISA’s Zero Trust Maturity Model organizes the work into five pillars: identity, devices, networks, applications and workloads, and data. Careers in IAM, endpoint security, and cloud security all grow from this shift.

Quantum and encryption

Today’s public-key encryption relies on math problems that ordinary computers cannot solve in any reasonable time. A large enough quantum computer could solve some of them, which would break much of the encryption the internet relies on. Attackers may already be collecting encrypted data to decrypt later. In August 2024 NIST published its first post-quantum cryptography standards, and organizations are now planning the long migration to them. That migration will need people who understand where encryption is used.

IoT security

Cameras, medical devices, building controls, and factory equipment are all on networks now, often with weak security. Operational technology (OT) security is a growing specialty; NIST added an OT Cybersecurity Engineering work role to the NICE Framework in 2025.

Skills that last

Tools change every few years. Fundamentals do not: networking, operating systems, how authentication works, scripting, writing clearly, and learning how to learn.
