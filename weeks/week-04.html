Explore

The CIA triad

Almost every security decision protects one of three things:

Confidentiality

Only the right people can see the data. Broken when grades leak or a password is stolen.

Integrity

Data is accurate and has not been tampered with. Broken when someone changes a bank transfer amount.

Availability

Systems and data are there when needed. Broken when ransomware locks a hospital’s records.

Common threats

Phishing tricks people into clicking a link, opening an attachment, or handing over a password, usually by email or text. Malware is any malicious software. Ransomware is malware that encrypts files and demands payment. Social engineering manipulates people rather than technology: a fake phone call from “IT,” a stranger following you through a locked door.

Most successful attacks start with a person, not a clever exploit. That is why security awareness is part of every IT job.

Common defenses

Multi-factor authentication (MFA) requires something besides a password, such as a code or an app prompt, and blocks most account takeovers. Firewalls filter traffic. A VPN encrypts traffic between a device and a trusted network. Backups, kept offline or separate, let an organization recover from ransomware without paying.

Ethics: the line that defines the profession

The tools a security professional uses to defend a network are often the same tools an attacker uses. What separates them is authorization: written permission from someone who owns the system. Testing a system you do not own without permission is illegal under laws such as the federal Computer Fraud and Abuse Act, even if you mean well.

Two more principles come up constantly. Privacy: IT staff can often see other people’s email and files, and professionals look only when the job requires it. Responsible disclosure: if you find a security flaw, you report it privately to the owner and give them time to fix it before telling anyone else.
