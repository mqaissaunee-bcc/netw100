Explore

Who works in tech

Workforce studies consistently find that women and several racial and ethnic groups are underrepresented in cybersecurity compared with the overall workforce. People also enter the field later in life, from the military, from other careers, and as first-generation college students. Organizations discuss representation for several reasons: widening the talent pool during a worker shortage, fairness and access, and the argument that teams with different experiences anticipate a wider range of threats and users. People debate how much weight each reason deserves and which programs work best, and you will hear a range of views in the industry.

Communities that help

WiCyS

Women in CyberSecurity: student chapters, conference scholarships, mentoring.

NSBE

National Society of Black Engineers: chapters and career fairs, including tech.

BDPA

A technology organization focused on Black professionals and students in IT.

Blacks in Technology

Community, mentoring, and events for Black technologists.

Many other groups exist for veterans, Latino and Hispanic technologists, LGBTQ+ professionals, people with disabilities, and career changers. Most welcome allies and anyone interested.

First-generation and returning students

If you are the first in your family to go to college, or returning after years away, Brookdale has support for you: advising, tutoring, counseling, accessibility services, and more. Using support is what successful students do, not a sign that you don’t belong.

Imposter syndrome

Imposter syndrome is the feeling that you are a fraud and will be “found out,” even when you are doing fine. It is extremely common in IT because the field is huge and changes fast; even senior engineers look things up all day. Practical responses: keep a list of things you have learned, compare yourself with where you were last month rather than with experts, and say “I don’t know yet, but I’ll find out,” which is exactly what employers want to hear.
