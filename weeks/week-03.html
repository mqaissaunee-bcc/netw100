Explore

Networks inside networks

A LAN (local area network) connects devices in one place: your home, a classroom, an office. A WAN (wide area network) connects LANs across distances: a company’s offices in Lincroft and Freehold. The internet is a network of networks, owned by thousands of companies that agree to pass each other’s traffic.

The devices

Switch

Connects devices inside a LAN and sends each message only to the device it is meant for.

Router

Connects different networks and picks the path traffic takes between them. Your home router connects your LAN to your internet provider.

Firewall

Allows or blocks traffic based on rules. Sits at the edge of a network like a security checkpoint.

Wireless access point

Lets Wi-Fi devices join the LAN. Home routers usually have one built in.

Addresses and names

Every device on a network has an IP address, like a mailing address, such as 142.250.80.46. People are bad at remembering numbers, so DNS (the Domain Name System) works like a contact list: you type google.com and DNS looks up the IP address for you.

Most devices also get their address automatically from DHCP when they join a network. That is why your phone just works when you join campus Wi-Fi.

The shift to the cloud

On-premises means the organization owns the servers in its own building. Cloud means renting computing from a provider like Microsoft Azure, Amazon Web Services, or Google Cloud. Most organizations now run a mix. The network still matters, because everything in the cloud is reached over a network. Network skills are moving toward cloud networking, not disappearing.

Why this matters for every role

The help desk troubleshoots Wi-Fi. The SOC analyst reads network traffic to spot attacks. The GRC analyst asks whether the firewall rules match policy. The cloud engineer builds virtual networks. Networking is the common language.
