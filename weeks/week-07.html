Explore

The security operations center (SOC)

A SOC is the team that watches for attacks. Alerts flow in from a SIEM (security information and event management) system and other tools.

Tier 1: triage

Work the alert queue. Separate false positives from real problems. Escalate with good notes. The most common entry-level security job.

Tier 2: incident response

Investigate escalated alerts, scope the damage, contain the threat.

Tier 3: threat hunting

Search for attackers who slipped past the alerts. Tune detections. Usually senior analysts.

Governance, risk, and compliance (GRC)

GRC professionals make sure security matches the rules the organization must follow and the risks it is willing to accept. They write policies, run risk assessments, prepare for audits, and map controls to standards such as the NIST Cybersecurity Framework, HIPAA, or PCI DSS. GRC rewards writing, organization, and communication, so it is a strong pathway for people coming from business, healthcare, law, or the military.

Ethical hacking

Penetration testers attack systems with written permission to find weaknesses before criminals do. The reality: a lot of planning, careful scoping, and report writing, not just breaking in. Most testers spend years in IT, networking, or the SOC first. Practice happens only in authorized labs such as TryHackMe or Hack The Box.

Certifications in this space

CompTIA Security+ is the most common baseline security certification and is required for many U.S. government and contractor roles. CompTIA CySA+ focuses on analyst work. ISC2 Certified in Cybersecurity (CC) is an entry-level credential. Its free training and exam program, One Million Certified in Cybersecurity, closed to new enrollments on May 20, 2026; the certification itself is still available.

Reading NICE: two categories

SOC and penetration testing work sit mostly in Protection and Defense (roles such as Defensive Cybersecurity, Incident Response, Threat Analysis, and Vulnerability Analysis). GRC work sits mostly in Oversight and Governance (roles such as Cybersecurity Policy and Planning, Security Control Assessment, and Privacy Compliance). Older sources call these “Protect and Defend” and “Oversee and Govern.”
